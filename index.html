Choosing verbs for research questions

The verb tells your committee what your design can deliver. Pick the design first, then a verb it can support, and keep the same population, setting and construct you named in your purpose statement.

Causal verbs need a causal design

Describewhat exists: levels, rates, frequencies
Comparegroups that already differ, with no claim about why
Relate · predictassociation, even with controls; still not cause
Changepre/post with a comparison group; cautious causal language
Affect · reducerandom assignment, or a strong quasi-experiment

“Impact,” “influence,” “effect,” “improve” and “narrow the gap” sit at the top rung. Use them only with a design that earns them.

Five tests of a strong question

  1. Traceable. It answers one phrase of your purpose statement.
  2. Specific. It names the population, setting, construct and, for quantitative work, how each variable is measured.
  3. Answerable. The data you can collect after IRB approval can actually answer it.
  4. Open where it should be. Qualitative questions begin with how or what, never yes or no.
  5. Honest. The verb promises no more than the design delivers, and the question doesn’t assume its answer.

Sharpening one question

Does mentoring impact teacher retention?

To what extent do first-year teachers’ ratings of mentor support predict their intent to return for a second year in a New Jersey K–12 district, controlling for school poverty level?

The revision names the measure, population, setting and control, and swaps a causal verb for one a correlational design supports.
